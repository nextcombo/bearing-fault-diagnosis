# Bearing Fault Diagnosis

Introduction: Modern industrial societies are highly dependent on various rotating machinery, such as motors and aircraft engines (Li et al., 2021a). Rolling bearings are indispensable core components of machines, becoming fragile and failing due to extreme working environments (Shen et al., 2021; Kumar et al., 2021). Once bearing failure occurs, the stability and reliability of rotating machinery are compromised, leading to economic losses and even casualties (Schwendemann et al., 2021; AlShorman et al., 2020). Therefore, it is imperative to design accurate and efficient bearing fault diagnosis systems.
Data-driven intelligent fault diagnosis (DIFD) has been a research mainstream in the past decade. The health condition of machines can be reflected in various data, such as acoustic signals (Glowacz et al., 2021), acoustic emission signals (AlShorman et al., 2021), thermal images (Shao et al., 2020), and vibration signals (Li et al., 2021). Among them, although acoustic signals have low measurement costs, they may contain sound information from other parts (Li et al., 2021d). Thermal images have the advantages of non-contact measurements and simple installation but are often expensive and cause significant computational burdens when processing image data (Glowacz, 2022). Vibration signals are the most commonly used data for bearing fault diagnosis. DIFD refers to the application of machine learning models in fault diagnosis (Lei et al., 2020). Traditional DIFD methods typically include two steps: feature extraction and fault classification. Specifically, experts first design statistical features sensitive to the given faults, followed by shallow machine learning methods (e.g., Support Vector Machines (Wei et al., 2020) or Adaptive Nearest Neighbor Reconstruction (Qian et al., 2022)) for distinguishing different health states of the machine. Although traditional DIFD methods have provided encouraging results in the early stages, there are some drawbacks. One disadvantage is that manually crafted features heavily rely on expert knowledge. Additionally, the weak learning capabilities of shallow learning methods cannot model the complex dynamics of the equipment.
Compared to traditional DIFD, deep learning-based DIFD can automatically learn feature processing from machine data in various complex scenarios. It can improve the performance of fault diagnosis systems and has become increasingly common in recent years. Extensive deep learning models such as Deep Belief Networks (Yang et al., 2021), Stacked Autoencoders (Shen et al., 2018), Transformers (Jin and Chen, 2021), Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) (Jia et al., 2022) have been applied to fault diagnosis. In particular, CNNs are widely studied for bearing fault diagnosis due to their competitive feature learning capabilities and significant scalability. The application of CNNs is very flexible. For example, Liu et al. (2019) proposed a multi-scale CNN using raw 1D vibration signals as input for detecting bearing faults under non-stationary operating conditions. Song et al. (2022) introduced a wide-kernel CNN for identifying the health status of bearings. In Wang et al. (2021 b), a multi-task 1D-CNN based on attention mechanism was developed for classifying bearing faults. In Zhang et al. (2018), a kernel-dropout 1D-CNN was designed for bearing diagnosis under different working loads.
Despite the good performance of CNNs in bearing fault diagnosis, extracting useful features from noisy vibration signals remains a critical issue for CNNs. Typically, accelerometers should be installed near the components of interest in the machine, and it is expected that sensing periodic pulses originating from fault points. However, vibration information from both the interested and uninterested components is collected by the data acquisition (DAQ) system (Pan et al., 2017). Unrelated sources of information may include background environment and components within the transmission path. According to the Central Limit Theorem, if the assumption is made that the vibration source is independent and the number of sources is large, additive vibrations from different sources will tend towards Gaussian noise signals when coupled (Han et al., 2021). When the power of Gaussian noise is sufficient, fault characteristics will inevitably be overwhelmed by unnecessary information, leading to low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Low SNR vibration signals make it difficult for discriminative features to be identified, especially for early faults with minor damage (Zhang et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2019). Unfortunately, in traditional CNNs, convolutional layers with trainable kernels are responsible for extracting features that represent faults, but there is no specific structure for denoising.
To enhance the robustness of CNNs to noise, several denoising strategies have been proposed for CNN models. For example, Liang et al. (2022) established a novel pooling layer based on Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) for denoising in ResNet models, which then input the wavelet transform images of vibration signals into the improved ResNet for diagnosing motor bearing faults. However, the computational complexity of SVD makes it inefficient during both training and inference stages. Peng et al. (2020) proposed a noise-robust CNN with multiple branches, one of which is a "denoiser branch" based on Gaussian filters. The CNN model performed well in wheelset bearing fault diagnosis, but the Gaussian filters used in the "denoiser branch" are not trainable, limiting its flexibility. In Zhang et al. (2017 b), a CNN model using wide kernels was introduced to suppress high-frequency noise in vibration signals, thereby improving performance. However, wide kernels are not a dedicated structure for denoising. Additionally, Wang et al. (2021a) applied joint learning to denoising by assisting the signal denoising task. However, this method relies on "noise-clean pairs" for training, which are always unknown in practice. This approach is limited in practical engineering applications due to the fact that actual clean signals are always unknown. In Li et al. (2021 b), a deep transient feature learning CNN model was constructed by recovering clean time-frequency distribution images from noisy images. Experimental results show that the CNN model can effectively suppress strong Gaussian noise and reveal repetitive pulses well. However, this model only suppresses noise and is not an end-to-end fault diagnosis model. Overall, there has been very little research work on enhancing the robustness of CNNs to noise, and a universal denoising CNN solution is crucial in this field and engineering practice.
Based on the above issues, this paper considers the periodic self-similarity of vibration signals and proposes a simple and effective noise suppression strategy based on Gramian, called Gramian Noise Reduction (GNR). In the proposed GNR method, the original 1D vibration signal is first shaped into a 2D raw signal matrix (RSM). The raw vectors in response surface methodology are segments of the vibration signal due to its inherent periodicity, which will exhibit strong similarity. Since the high similarity of raw vectors, RSM should be a low-rank matrix (Du et al., 2021). Generally speaking, Gaussian noise will enhance the dense patterns in RSM by contaminating the effective periodic pulses in fault signals, weakening the low-rank characteristics of RSM. Therefore, we derive a two-dimensional data matrix multiplied by its Gramian, and obtain an enhanced signal matrix (ESM), whose low-rank properties are improved. After normalization, ESM is flattened back to a one-dimensional form, where unnecessary noise is greatly suppressed. As a result, the meaningful periodic pulse components are enhanced. The idea behind GNR is based on the low-rank assumption of RSM with periodic self-similarity for vibration signals (Du et al., 2021). The basic principle of GNR denoising is to enhance the low-rank patterns (related to useful feature information) in RSM and suppress the dense patterns (related to noise).
Additionally, Gramian Time-Frequency Enhancement Network (GTFENet), a new end-to-end CNN model equipped with GNR for bearing fault diagnosis. In GTFE-Net, there are three parallel branches: the raw signal branch (RSB), the time enhancement branch (TEB), and the frequency enhancement branch (FEB). The inputs of RSB, TEB, and FEB are the raw vibration signal, the GNR denoised signal, and the spectrum, respectively. With the help of GNR, convolutional layers in GTFE-Net can focus more on feature extraction rather than noise suppression. To simplify implementation and enhance flexibility, a modular design was adopted. The above three branches RSB, TEB, and FEB form three modules in the depth direction. These three modules are branch generation module (BGM), feature learning module (FLM), and fault classification module (FCM). BGM outputs the feature maps of the three branches. FLM is used to learn features from the feature maps. FCM has a SoftMax layer for fault classification. By stacking one BGM, multiple FLMs, and one FLM, GTFE-Net is constructed. The contributions of this file are summarized as follows:
(1) This paper proposes a novel Gramian-based denoising strategy called GNR. This denoising method can significantly reduce useless noise and interference. GNR can enhance valuable feature information in vibration signals through low-order enhancement. The proposed GNR does not require parameters and is easy to calculate, making it flexible to integrate into different network architectures.
(2) Then, a new end-to-end CNN model with a denoising structure called GTFE-Net was developed for fault signal diagnosis. GTFE-Net contains three branches: RSB, TEB, and FEB. RSB ensures that no information loss occurs due to denoising. A Gramian bridge between RSB and TEB can denoise the feature maps of the middle layer of RSB. We also introduce a frequency domain representation in FEB to further improve the model's feature extraction capability.
(3) GTFE-Net adopts a modular design. Three modules, including BGM, FLM, and FCM, are constructed to implement the three branches of GTFE-Net. The modular design simplifies complex branch interactions and provides great flexibility for other applications.
(4) Measurement experiments were conducted on a real engineering dataset and two test bench bearing datasets, including erosion studies, visualization analysis, and comparative analysis. The results show that GNR can effectively enhance the periodic components in vibration signals, and GTFE-Net's classification performance is superior to SOTA methods in high noise backgrounds.
